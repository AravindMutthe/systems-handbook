# How the global technology industry works

A front guide to the Systems Handbook. Read this first, then follow the learning order from the ground base upward.

No single controller, no single mandatory sequence

No single government, corporation, or standards organization controls the entire system. Different players control different parts, with different kinds of power: legal authority, ownership, technical influence, market share, or purchasing power.

A player can sit in several layers at once. A government is a law-maker, an industrial-policy maker, and a customer. A cloud platform is a service provider and a participant in standards work. A bank is a customer and, through what it buys, a shaper of the market.

A reference sequence, not a universal rule

There is a common technology commercialization and adoption lifecycle. It is a useful reference sequence, not a universal chronological rule. Standardization, regulation, development, manufacturing and adoption frequently happen in parallel or repeat in cycles.

1. Need or demand — Businesses, governments or consumers identify a problem. Example: banks need faster transaction processing. 2. Research and development — Researchers and companies develop possible solutions. Example: developing digital communications. 3. Standards and coordination — Industry groups establish interoperability rules. Example: Ethernet and TCP/IP specifications. 4. Manufacturing and infrastructure — Suppliers build equipment and operators deploy systems. Example: NICs, switches, fiber networks. 5. Regulation and certification — Applicable authorities enforce legal and safety requirements. Example: spectrum licensing or equipment approvals. 6. Commercial services — Businesses package technology into products and services. Example: internet access, cloud hosting, digital payments. 7. Industrial adoption — Organizations integrate services into their operations. Example: hospitals adopting connected information systems.

For example, the Internet was deployed and evolved while many of its protocols were still being standardized.

Newcomers can enter at different points

A newcomer does not have to start at the beginning of that sequence. Entry point depends on business model, and entry point determines which gates apply. It does not remove governance.

NewcomerPossible entry pointMain requirements
Software startupBuild applications using existing APIsSecurity, licenses, applicable data laws

Network equipment manufacturerDesign and manufacture hardwareTechnical standards, certification where required
Telecom operatorBuild and operate communications networksLicensing, spectrum rights where applicable
Semiconductor companyDesign chips using existing manufacturing partnersIP rights, design tools, fabrication access, export rules

A software startup doesn't need to manufacture silicon or invent TCP/IP before entering the market. It can build on the infrastructure and standards that already exist.

However, a telecom operator cannot simply ignore applicable licensing requirements.

Three sequences to follow, and four perspectives

The library can be read through three interconnected sequences. Their arrows indicate different kinds of relationships, not necessarily strict dependencies.

  • Historical evolution — How technology changed over time. Telegraph, Telephone, Digital networks, Internet and AI.
  • Technical dependency — Which resources and interfaces support other components. Hardware, Drivers and OS, Network stack, Applications.
  • Market and governance relationships — Who creates, regulates, supplies, operates and consumes technology. Governance, Standards, Industry, Market.

Together, four perspectives form the foundation of this library:

  • Historical sequence tells us what happened.
  • Technical dependencies explain how systems work.
  • Governance and market structures explain who influences what happens.
  • Business strategy explains why organizations choose particular paths.

The arrows run both ways, and the process feeds back

Governments influence industries, but industries also influence standards, investment, regulation and technological development. Influence is not one-way, and it is not a single arrow. A government regulates an industry. An industry proposes standards, invests in technology development, and, through its choices and its procurement, shapes what gets built next.

And the process feeds back into itself: adoption creates new demands, which influence the next generation of technology. The library does not end at adoption. Adoption leads back to a new need, and a new cycle begins.

That is why this approach should not be limited to a history textbook or a networking textbook. It can become an interconnected study of: how human and business needs create demand, how governments and industries shape markets, how standards and technologies develop, how infrastructure is built, how organizations adopt it, and how new business models emerge.

Preserving the historical chronology, while examining these relationships, lets us examine, for example, why the United States developed particular internet institutions, how India's telecom and digital infrastructure evolved, how semiconductor supply chains became globally interconnected, and why different industries adopted technologies at different times.
