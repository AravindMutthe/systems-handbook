# How the global technology industry works

A front guide to the Systems Handbook. Read this first, then
follow the learning order from the ground base upward.

## No single controller, no single mandatory sequence

No single government, corporation, or standards organization
controls the entire system. Different players control different
parts, with different kinds of power: legal authority, ownership,
technical influence, market share, or purchasing power.

A player can sit in several layers at once. A government is a
law-maker, an industrial-policy maker, and a customer. A cloud
platform is a service provider and a participant in standards
work. A bank is a customer and, through what it buys, a shaper
of the market.

## Who influences the global technology market

| Layer | Major players | What they influence |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Governments | US, India, EU, China | Laws, trade, security, spectrum, industrial policy |
| International coordination | ITU, WTO | Telecommunications coordination, international trade rules |
| Technical standards | IEEE, ISO/IEC, IETF, 3GPP | Interoperability, protocols, technical specifications |
| Internet coordination | ICANN, IANA functions, RIRs | Domain names, IP address allocation and registries |
| Semiconductor supply chain | ASML, TSMC, Intel, NVIDIA | Equipment, fabrication, processors, computing capabilities |
| Network infrastructure | Cisco, Ericsson, Nokia, telecom operators | Network equipment, connectivity, deployment |
| Software platforms | Operating systems, databases, APIs, applications | The layer applications are built on and run on |
| Cloud and platforms | AWS, Microsoft, Google | Infrastructure services, platforms, ecosystems |
| Industries and customers | Banks, hospitals, manufacturers, governments | Demand, procurement requirements, practical adoption |
| New businesses and market opportunities | Builders on existing infrastructure | Solving customer problems and creating new value |

## A reference sequence, not a universal rule

There is a common technology commercialization and adoption
lifecycle. It is a useful reference sequence, not a universal
chronological rule. Standardization, regulation, development,
manufacturing and adoption frequently happen in parallel or
repeat in cycles.

1. Need or demand — Businesses, governments or consumers
identify a problem. Example: banks need faster transaction
processing.
2. Research and development — Researchers and companies
develop possible solutions. Example: developing digital
communications.
3. Standards and coordination — Industry groups establish
interoperability rules. Example: Ethernet and TCP/IP
specifications.
4. Manufacturing and infrastructure — Suppliers build
equipment and operators deploy systems. Example: NICs,
switches, fiber networks.
5. Regulation and certification — Applicable authorities
enforce legal and safety requirements. Example: spectrum
licensing or equipment approvals.
6. Commercial services — Businesses package technology
into products and services. Example: internet access,
cloud hosting, digital payments.
7. Industrial adoption — Organizations integrate services
into their operations. Example: hospitals adopting
connected information systems.

For example, the Internet was deployed and evolved while many
of its protocols were still being standardized.

## Newcomers can enter at different points

A newcomer does not have to start at the beginning of that
sequence. Entry point depends on business model, and entry
point determines which gates apply. It does not remove
governance.

| Newcomer | Possible entry point | Main requirements |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Software startup | Build applications using existing APIs | Security, licenses, applicable data laws |
| Cloud services company | Resell or manage existing infrastructure | Contracts, technical capability, service obligations |
| Network equipment manufacturer | Design and manufacture hardware | Technical standards, certification where required |
| Telecom operator | Build and operate communications networks | Licensing, spectrum rights where applicable |
| Semiconductor company | Design chips using existing manufacturing partners | IP rights, design tools, fabrication access, export rules |

A software startup doesn't need to manufacture silicon or
invent TCP/IP before entering the market. It can build on
the infrastructure and standards that already exist.

However, a telecom operator cannot simply ignore applicable
licensing requirements.

## Three sequences to follow, and four perspectives

The library can be read through three interconnected
sequences. Their arrows indicate different kinds of
relationships, not necessarily strict dependencies.

- Historical evolution — How technology changed over time.
  Telegraph, Telephone, Digital networks, Internet and AI.
- Technical dependency — Which resources and interfaces
  support other components. Hardware, Drivers and OS,
  Network stack, Applications.
- Market and governance relationships — Who creates,
  regulates, supplies, operates and consumes technology.
  Governance, Standards, Industry, Market.

Together, four perspectives form the foundation of this
library:

- Historical sequence tells us what happened.
- Technical dependencies explain how systems work.
- Governance and market structures explain who influences
  what happens.
- Business strategy explains why organizations choose
  particular paths.

## The arrows run both ways, and the process feeds back

Governments influence industries, but industries also
influence standards, investment, regulation and
technological development. Influence is not one-way, and
it is not a single arrow. A government regulates an
industry. An industry proposes standards, invests in
technology development, and, through its choices and its
procurement, shapes what gets built next.

And the process feeds back into itself: adoption creates
new demands, which influence the next generation of
technology. The library does not end at adoption. Adoption
leads back to a new need, and a new cycle begins.

That is why this approach should not be limited to a
history textbook or a networking textbook. It can become
an interconnected study of: how human and business needs
create demand, how governments and industries shape
markets, how standards and technologies develop, how
infrastructure is built, how organizations adopt it, and
how new business models emerge.

Preserving the historical chronology, while examining
these relationships, lets us examine, for example, why
the United States developed particular internet
institutions, how India's telecom and digital
infrastructure evolved, how semiconductor supply chains
became globally interconnected, and why different
industries adopted technologies at different times.
