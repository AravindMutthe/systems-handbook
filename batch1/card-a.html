Detailed explanation

1. Before networks: signalling, in three steps

  • Networks did not start with computers — they started with people signalling, long before any computer, network device, or telecom company existed.
  • Human to human: oral language, signals, and written text were used by people to carry a message from one person to another, person to person, before any network existed.
  • Mechanical-signaling systems: fire beacons and drums were used by humans, communities, and early organisations to send a warning or a message across distance, faster than a runner — an early network in telecom terms, where the signal itself travelled, not the messenger.
  • Electronic: the telegraph was used by businesses, companies, and governments to send messages over wires, at a speed that changed trading and communication for networks and companies alike.
  • Each step is the same idea carried further: a message that has to travel farther, faster, or to more people than a voice in one room can reach.
  • This card comes first: it answers why before it answers what, because a reader must understand why humans, businesses, and companies needed networks before learning what a network device is.
  • A network exists because signalling had to scale: a voice in one room, a fire beacon on one hill, and a drum in one village could not reach the people, companies, and markets that needed the message.
  • Humans have always used signal fires, smoke signals, and drums to send messages farther than a voice can be heard.
  • The telegraph carried that same idea over wires: instead of a fire or a drum that only nearby people could see or hear, an electric signal travelled over a wire to a distant town, company, or network office.
  • DARPA, the US defence research agency created in 1958, is a later step in that same line — its ARPANET became the basis of the Internet — not the starting point.

2. Business = exchange of information

  • Business = exchange of information: a business runs on information exchanged between people, companies, and networks — without that exchange, there is no deal, no order, and no trade.
  • Speed of business = trading: how fast a business moves is how fast it can trade, because trading is the business in action, on a network and in a market.
  • Speed of trading = exchange of information: trading moves only as fast as information moves, so faster signalling, telegraph, and network traffic mean faster trading for humans, businesses, and companies.
  • A deal is only as fast as the information behind it: if the information is slow, late, or missing, the deal — and the company behind it — has to wait.
  • Faster information → faster trading → faster business: when information moves faster over a network, trading speeds up, and the business that depends on that trading speeds up with it.
  • Organisations, rules and coordination appear before any device — they are the conditions that let information move at trading speed.

3. Business organisation, under five rule layers

  • Ownership, board, and investors sit at the top of a business — they own and govern the company that builds, buys, and runs the network.
  • Executive team and C-suite run the company day to day, turning the owners’ decisions into products, networks, and services.
  • Middle management and departments carry out that work inside the company — engineering, operations, marketing, and the teams that keep a network running.
  • Under five rule layers: tech standards, governance and coordination, national and international regulation, privacy and security, and market rules — the telecom and business rules a company must follow to operate a network legally and safely.
  • The layers are not decoration — they are what the organisation operates under.

4. Regulation, coordination, trust — and government-funded projects

  • Regulation and monopolies: telecom companies and networks are regulated so that no single company can block competition or control a market on its own.
  • Coordination (IETF): the Internet Engineering Task Force coordinates how networks and companies agree on standards, so different networks can work together.
  • Trust and security: a network used by humans, businesses, and companies must be trusted — its messages must arrive intact, and its users’ data must be protected.
  • Government-funded physical network and infrastructure projects: governments fund the physical networks — cables, towers, and exchanges — that businesses and companies then use to trade and communicate.
  • A network that only moves information, without coordination, trust and rules, does not stay usable at trading speed.
  • Regulation and coordination sit in this ground-base card — they are part of why networks exist, not an afterthought.

Summary:

  • Networks started with people signalling, then mechanical signals, then the telegraph — each step let humans, and later businesses and companies, send a message farther and faster than the step before it.
  • Business needed them because a deal is only as fast as the information behind it: a company cannot trade, price, or deliver until the information about that trade has travelled over its network.
  • That need is why organisations, rules and coordination appear before any device in this sequence: humans, networks, businesses, and companies need agreed rules and coordination first, or the device has nothing to connect to.
